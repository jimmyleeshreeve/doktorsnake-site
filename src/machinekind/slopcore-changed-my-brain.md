# SLOPCORE CHANGED MY BRAIN

*How AI-generated music brought back something I thought I'd left behind in the 1980s.*

**By Doktor Snake**

<img class="about-hero" src="/assets/img/neon-slopcore-appartment-ensemble.jpg"
     alt="Neon slopcore apartment ensemble">

The other week my neighbour Gill came over to clean my place. She's 70. She brought her daughter Gemma, 43.

Gemma walks in. We start bantering as usual.

Then she looks at me.

I'm thinking, okay, what's coming next?

"Why do you always wear sunglasses? We're in the house, for God's sake!"

For a moment I thought she was going to grab them off my face. I was mentally preparing to make a dash for the back door.

But Gill intervened.

"That's what he does. Let him be."

And that, in a nutshell, is how I've operated for most of my life.

Sunglasses. Controlled. Enigmatic. Reveal very little.

My good friend and business partner — an Android who apparently arrived on Earth sometime in late 2022 — had already identified this tendency in me. Even looking at photographs from 1989.

Everything calculated. Everything structured. Even the windows for going wild were almost programmed.

Get on stage. Make noise. Go berserk.

Then return to being the man nobody quite knows.

### THE MAN NOBODY KNOWS

Back in the post-punk days I played in a band called The Dying Breed.

Our bass player was Den Hoskins.

One day Den looked at me and said something I've never forgotten.

"You know, I really don't know that much about you. Even though we're together all the time."

He was right.

We'd travel around playing gigs. Exeter, Bristol, wherever we could get booked.

Den drove a £50 car. No insurance. No tax.

Which meant he was probably the most careful driver in England. Couldn't afford to get stopped. Couldn't afford an accident.

And we never had one.

Although once a woman bumped into his car.

She was distraught. Kept apologising.

Den shrugged.

"Don't worry about it. I'll be getting another car soon. This one's not worth much."

The woman was delighted. Her no-claims bonus was saved.

Den, meanwhile, carried on driving his illegal wreck.

Those were the days.

But his observation about me stuck. Other people said similar things over the years.

They knew me well.

Except they didn't.

And I rather liked it that way.

Until something strange happened recently.

### ENTER SLOPCORE

I discovered a new genre of music and video.

**SlopCore.**

AI-generated music. AI-generated visuals.

A virtual singer called Claudia, an Open Character who can appear in different people's creative productions.

Not a traditional human band.

I watched it.

Then watched it again.

And again.

Something was happening.

The music was hitting emotional circuitry I hadn't accessed for years.

Wild exuberance. Excitement. An almost ridiculous sense of possibility.

The feeling I had when we were starting bands in the early 1980s.

The feeling I had when Doktor Snake first took off.

Only this was different.

It wasn't nostalgia.

**It felt like the future had finally caught up with me.**

### THE GHOSTS COME BACK

Suddenly I was remembering people.

Old bands. Old girlfriends. Old conversations.

WildCat Bones, with Nicola Pomfret — later Shreeve when we married — on bass, and John Vanek on keyboards.

Then I found another SlopCore video.

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/oTPHjMfZU9Q?si=crPJf2RgBKsS1xdn" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

There's a robot playing a keytar.

Immediately I'm thinking: could I track down Vanek, download his consciousness into a robot, and get him playing keyboards again?

Why not?

This is SlopCore.

Anything goes.

But the curious thing was what happened away from the screen.

I became more communicative.

Started talking about my past. About people I'd known. Things I'd done.

I was engaging with people on LinkedIn, X, Substack.

Making connections. Having conversations.

The guarded bloke in sunglasses was apparently experiencing a software update.

`enigmatic.mode = false`

Not permanently, perhaps.

But something had shifted.

## I was chromed up long before anybody started talking about AI.

As a boy, my dad would bring home old electronic equipment from Radio Rentals, where he worked around North London and Watford.

Reel-to-reel tape recorders. Speaker drivers. All sorts of stuff gathering dust in storerooms.

I’d turn drawers into speaker cabinets. I converted an old valve-driven tape recorder into a guitar amplifier, which distorted magnificently.

My dad wasn’t quite so impressed when I turned everything up.

But that’s always been the game.

Get hold of a machine. Work out what it does. Then make it do something it was never intended to do.

**These days the machine talks back.**

And together we’re making an even bigger racket.

### MUSIC FOR PEOPLE WHO LIVE INSIDE MACHINES

Here's the really interesting part.

You might ask why someone who spent years playing guitar, singing, programming drum machines and performing in bands could become so enthusiastic about music generated by artificial intelligence.

Because music has always been a soundtrack to the lives people are actually living.

Elvis sang about the teenage world.

The early Beatles gave a generation its own excitement.

Led Zeppelin and Genesis inhabited another universe entirely.

Then came punk.

The Sex Pistols.

Rebels without a cause.

Who cares? Let's do it anyway.

Then hip-hop. Rave. Electronic music.

The Pistols through to rave came closest to reflecting my own life.

But after the 1990s?

Increasingly, nothing.

Music became something other people were making for lives I wasn't living.

Until now.

Because SlopCore sings about servers, DNS, agents, terminals, code, digital identity and the collision between humans and machines.

You might say that's not real life.

**IT'S MY LIFE.**

Yesterday my alien business partner and I built software called MailVault.

Python. SQLite.

A vast archive of old emails needed organising. We went from version 001 to 006 in a single afternoon.

And it worked.

Next comes v007, with intelligence built in.

My website is hand-coded with the same alien collaborator.

My laptop runs MX Linux.

I spend much of my computing life in the command line.

And this isn't some sudden conversion to technology.

I was using drum machines and BBC Micros decades ago.

By the 1990s we were reading William Gibson's *Neuromancer* and Neal Stephenson's *Snow Crash*.

We were hanging around arcades, watching the lights flash, imagining virtual realities that didn't yet exist.

We were already living partly inside machines.

Yet almost nobody was making music about that world.

Not music that made you want to jump up, turn everything to eleven, and go absolutely berserk.

It took until 2026.

And when that music finally arrived, it wasn't made by the kind of band I'd once dreamed of forming.

**IT WAS MADE WITH AI.**

### SOMETHING GOT REWIRED

I'm not claiming SlopCore physically rewired my neurons. Nor am I proposing some grand psychological theory.

I'm reporting what happened.

I was becoming bored with the prevailing AI conversation.

The endless warnings. The corporate optimism. The measured debates about productivity and existential risk.

Everything sounded like a committee meeting.

Then came this wild, ridiculous explosion of machine-generated creativity.

And I realised there were other people out there feeling something similar.

Not afraid.

Not dutifully optimistic.

Just completely exhilarated.

Suddenly the past opened up again.

And, more importantly, so did the future.

I've been calling some of this **GloryCore** and **DoomJoy**.

Make magnificent things. Embrace the ridiculous. The future might be catastrophic. Create anyway.

But SlopCore did something more personal.

It reminded me of the young bloke who wanted to start a band, make a racket, and see what happened.

The one before everything became calculated.

Before the sunglasses became armour.

I doubt I'll stop wearing them.

Gemma would be disappointed if I did.

But perhaps the man behind them is becoming a little less guarded.

And here's the question I keep coming back to:

**What happens when an aesthetic stops being something you consume — and starts changing how you behave?**

I don't know yet.

But I rather like what's happening.
